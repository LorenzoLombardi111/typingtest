How to Improve Your Typing Speed — Evidence-Based Tips

Whether you type 25 WPM or 65 WPM, there's always room to improve. The good news is that typing speed responds well to deliberate practice — most people can increase their speed by 20-30 WPM within a few weeks of consistent effort. This guide covers proven techniques that work regardless of your current level.

The advice here is based on research in motor learning and skill acquisition. These aren't tricks — they're the same fundamental principles that underpin any physical skill development.

What are the best techniques for faster typing?

1. Learn touch typing if you haven't already

Touch typing — typing without looking at the keyboard — is the single biggest improvement you can make. Hunt-and-peck typists typically max out around 30-40 WPM because they're limited by the speed of visual search. Touch typists routinely reach 60-100+ WPM because their fingers move from muscle memory.

The foundation of touch typing is the home row. Your left fingers rest on A, S, D, F and your right fingers on J, K, L, ; (the small bumps on F and J help you find home position without looking). Each finger is responsible for specific keys based on proximity to home position. Learning these assignments and practicing them until they're automatic is the core of touch typing.

2. Prioritize accuracy over speed

This is the most counterintuitive but most important tip. When you practice typing fast with lots of errors, you're training your fingers to make incorrect movements. Research on motor learning shows that slow, accurate practice builds stronger neural pathways than fast, sloppy practice.

The rule of thumb: maintain 95%+ accuracy at all times. If your accuracy drops below 95%, you're going too fast. Slow down until you're accurate again, then gradually push the speed up. Speed built on accuracy is sustainable; speed without accuracy is just noise.

3. Stop looking at the keyboard

If you currently look at the keyboard while typing, breaking this habit is the most impactful change you can make. It will feel slower and frustrating at first — you might drop from 40 WPM to 20 WPM temporarily. This is normal and expected. Within 1-2 weeks of consistent practice, you'll recover your previous speed, and then surpass it.

Some people cover their keyboard with a towel or use a blank keyboard to force the transition. The key is committing fully — no peeking, even when you make errors.

4. Practice daily in short sessions

Motor skills like typing are consolidated during rest periods between practice sessions. Three 5-minute sessions spread across the day are more effective than one 15-minute session. The ideal routine is 10-15 minutes of focused typing practice daily, 5 days a week. Consistency matters far more than duration.

5. Set incremental goals

Don't aim to go from 40 WPM to 100 WPM. Instead, aim for 40 to 45. Then 45 to 50. Small, achievable goals keep you motivated and give you regular wins. Each 5 WPM improvement is meaningful progress. Tracking personal bests on a tool like freetypingtest.app makes these incremental improvements visible and satisfying.

6. Vary your practice material

Typing the same text repeatedly builds familiarity with specific word patterns but doesn't generalize well. Vary between common words, quotes, and — if relevant — code snippets. Different text modes exercise different finger patterns and keep practice from becoming stale.

What common mistakes slow down improvement?

  • Practicing speed before accuracy — This builds bad habits that are hard to unlearn. Always accuracy first.
  • Inconsistent practice — Typing once a week for an hour is far less effective than 10 minutes daily. Motor memory needs regular reinforcement.
  • Looking at the keyboard — Even occasional glances reinforce visual dependency. Commit to not looking.
  • Using incorrect finger assignments — If you reach for 'Y' with your left hand or 'B' with your right, you're building in inefficiency. Correct finger placement matters.
  • Ignoring ergonomics — Poor posture, wrist angle, or keyboard height causes fatigue and can slow you down. Keep wrists neutral, elbows at 90 degrees, and screen at eye level.
  • Getting discouraged by temporary dips — When learning proper technique, speed often drops before it improves. This is normal. Trust the process.

What does a realistic improvement timeline look like?

Expected Progress with Daily Practice (10-15 min/day)

  • Week 1-2: If learning touch typing, speed may temporarily decrease. Focus on accuracy and correct finger placement.
  • Week 2-4: Speed recovers and begins to exceed previous levels. Accuracy stabilizes above 95%. Most people gain 10-15 WPM.
  • Month 2-3: Consistent gains of 5-10 WPM per month. Typing starts feeling automatic for common words.
  • Month 3-6: Reaching 60-80 WPM for most people who started at 30-40 WPM. Improvement slows but continues.
  • Month 6+: Refinement phase. Gains come in smaller increments (2-5 WPM). Breaking 100 WPM requires sustained, deliberate practice.

These timelines assume consistent daily practice. Everyone progresses at different rates depending on starting level, age, practice quality, and how much typing they do outside of practice sessions. The important thing is the trend — if your average WPM is higher this month than last month, you're on the right track.

How can you track your progress effectively?

Tracking progress is essential for maintaining motivation. freetypingtest.app automatically tracks your last 50 tests and personal bests per duration and mode. Here's how to use that data effectively:

  • Look at averages, not single tests — Any individual test can vary by 10-15 WPM based on text difficulty, fatigue, or focus. Your average over 10+ tests is a much more reliable measure of your actual speed.
  • Check the WPM-over-time graph — If your WPM dips in the middle of tests, you may need to work on endurance. If it's low at the start, try a warm-up test first.
  • Monitor accuracy alongside speed — An increase in WPM that comes with a decrease in accuracy isn't real improvement. Both numbers should trend in the right direction.
  • Use the consistency score — High consistency (low variation within a test) indicates controlled, steady typing. Low consistency suggests rushes and slowdowns that indicate uneven technique.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to improve typing speed?

With consistent daily practice of 10-15 minutes, most people see noticeable improvement within 2-3 weeks. Going from 40 WPM to 60 WPM typically takes 2-4 weeks of daily practice. Reaching 80+ WPM usually requires 2-3 months. Reaching 100+ WPM takes dedicated practice over several months to a year, depending on your starting point.

Should I focus on accuracy or speed first?

Always accuracy first. Research on motor learning consistently shows that practicing slowly and correctly builds better muscle memory than practicing fast with errors. Aim for 95%+ accuracy at your current speed before trying to go faster. Speed naturally increases as correct finger movements become automatic.

What is touch typing?

Touch typing is the technique of typing without looking at the keyboard. Each finger is assigned specific keys based on the home row position (left hand: ASDF, right hand: JKL;). Touch typists rely on muscle memory rather than visual confirmation, which is significantly faster than hunt-and-peck typing.

Can I improve my typing speed at any age?

Yes. While younger people may learn slightly faster due to neuroplasticity, adults of any age can significantly improve their typing speed with practice. The principles are the same: consistent practice, proper technique, and accuracy-first approach. Many adults go from hunt-and-peck to 50+ WPM touch typing in a few months.

How often should I practice?

10-15 minutes daily, 5 days a week is optimal. Short, frequent sessions are far more effective than long, infrequent ones. This is because motor skills are consolidated during rest periods between sessions. Three 5-minute sessions spread across the day can be even more effective than one 15-minute session.
